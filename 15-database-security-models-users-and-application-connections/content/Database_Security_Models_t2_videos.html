Introduction

Every database engine assumes that not everyone who connects should be able to do everything. User accounts identify who is connecting, privileges define what each account is allowed to do, and roles bundle privileges into reusable packages that can be granted and revoked as job responsibilities change. In this topic you will look at how accounts are created with CREATE USER, how access rights are assigned with GRANT and withdrawn with REVOKE, and how a role-based access control (RBAC) model keeps permission management manageable as the number of users grows.

Two ideas sit underneath the mechanics. The principle of least privilege says each account should hold only the rights it needs for its task and nothing more, so that a compromised or careless account causes limited damage. Separation of duties says that sensitive combinations of power — for example, the ability to both change data and erase the audit trail of that change — should not sit in a single role, and that administrative accounts should be distinct from everyday application accounts.

The videos below approach this from two directions: one conceptual video on access control models, and two hands-on walkthroughs in MySQL and Oracle. While watching the conceptual video, pay attention to how roles differ from attribute-based rules and when each approach fits. While watching the hands-on videos, note the exact syntax for creating an account, the difference between creating a user and granting that user any privileges, and how the demonstrated grants would change if you deliberately restricted them to the minimum needed.

An IBM Technology explainer that contrasts role-based access control with attribute-based access control. Expect a definition of RBAC in terms of roles, permissions assigned to roles, and users assigned to roles, followed by ABAC's use of attributes such as user, resource, and environment characteristics to evaluate access decisions, along with trade-offs between the two models.

Why this video
