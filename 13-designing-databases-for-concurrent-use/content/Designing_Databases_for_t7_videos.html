Introduction

When many users hit the same database at the same time, the system needs a strategy for deciding what happens when two transactions want the same row. The three broad families of answers are pessimistic concurrency control (lock the data first and make others wait), optimistic concurrency control (let transactions proceed and check for conflicts at commit time, usually with a version number or timestamp), and multiversion concurrency control (keep multiple versions of each row so readers never block writers and writers never block readers). Most modern systems, including PostgreSQL, combine these ideas rather than using just one.

This matters in COP4708 because isolation levels, locking behavior, and deadlocks are not abstract theory — they are the direct consequence of which strategy your database engine uses and how you write your transactions. Choosing the wrong approach for your workload shows up as lost updates and corrupted data on one extreme, or as lock contention, timeouts, and sluggish throughput on the other. The practical goal is consistency that does not cost you performance, which means matching the strategy to the read/write mix and conflict rate of your application.

As you watch, keep a comparison table in mind: for each strategy, note who waits, when the conflict is detected, what happens when a conflict occurs (block, retry, or abort), and what the cost is (lock overhead, retry overhead, or version storage and cleanup). Also watch for the classic motivating example — two users buying the last item — and ask yourself which strategy you would choose if conflicts are rare versus frequent, and how you would monitor the result in production.
