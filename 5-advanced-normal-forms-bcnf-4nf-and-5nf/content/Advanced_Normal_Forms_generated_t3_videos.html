Introduction

Third normal form (3NF) and Boyce-Codd normal form (BCNF) are close cousins. Both attack the same problem — update, insertion, and deletion anomalies caused by functional dependencies on something other than a whole key — but they draw the line in slightly different places. 3NF allows a dependency whose left-hand side is not a superkey as long as the attribute on the right is prime (part of some candidate key); BCNF allows no such exception, requiring every non-trivial functional dependency to have a superkey on the left. For the large majority of schemas, these two definitions select the same decompositions, and the distinction never comes up in practice.

The interesting cases are the ones where they diverge. The classic gap appears when a relation has multiple overlapping candidate keys — the textbook examples usually involve something like a course, an instructor, and a time slot. There, the schema can be in 3NF yet still carry redundancy, and pushing it to BCNF forces you to split a relation in a way that leaves some functional dependency spanning two tables, so it can no longer be checked without a join. That is the trade-off at the heart of this topic: BCNF buys you stronger redundancy elimination, while 3NF guarantees that a dependency-preserving decomposition always exists. Both forms, importantly, can always be achieved with a lossless join.

As you watch, keep three questions in front of you for every example: what are all the candidate keys, which functional dependencies have a non-superkey on the left, and after a proposed decomposition can each original dependency still be enforced inside a single relation? Note also that normalization is a design judgment, not a rule to apply blindly — be ready to argue when stopping at 3NF is the right engineering choice and when the redundancy BCNF removes is worth the cost of enforcing a dependency across tables.
