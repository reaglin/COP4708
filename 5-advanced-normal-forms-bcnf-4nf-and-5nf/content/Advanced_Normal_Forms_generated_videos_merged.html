Review of 3NF and Its Limitations

Introduction

Third Normal Form is the design target most students meet first, and for good reason: a relation in 3NF has no partial dependencies and no transitive dependencies on non-prime attributes, which removes most of the update, insertion, and deletion anomalies that plague unnormalized tables. But 3NF contains a deliberate loophole. Its definition forgives a functional dependency when the attribute on the right-hand side is prime — that is, part of some candidate key — even if the determinant on the left is not a superkey. That exception is where redundancy can survive normalization.

In this topic we revisit the 3NF definition carefully and then push on that loophole. The classic trouble case involves overlapping candidate keys: when two or more candidate keys share attributes, a non-superkey determinant can legitimately exist inside a 3NF relation, and repeated facts (along with the anomalies they cause) come back. We also look at the trade-off that motivated the loophole in the first place, because 3NF decomposition can always be made both lossless and dependency-preserving, while stricter forms sometimes force you to give up dependency preservation.

As you watch, keep three questions in front of you. First, for each functional dependency shown, is the left-hand side a superkey, and if not, why does 3NF still allow it? Second, where exactly does the redundancy appear in the sample rows, and what anomaly would it cause on insert, update, or delete? Third, what is given up when a relation is decomposed further — can every original dependency still be checked within a single resulting table? Those questions carry directly into BCNF, 4NF, and 5NF later in the module.

Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)

Introduction

Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF) is a stricter version of Third Normal Form. Where 3NF tolerates certain functional dependencies whose left-hand sides are not keys (as long as the right-hand side is a prime attribute), BCNF removes that exception entirely: for every nontrivial functional dependency X -> Y in the relation, X must be a superkey. That single rule is the whole definition, but applying it requires you to be comfortable finding candidate keys and listing the functional dependencies that actually hold in a schema.

This matters in COP4708 because BCNF is the practical stopping point for most relational designs built on functional dependencies. Relations that satisfy 3NF but not BCNF can still carry redundancy and update anomalies, and these cases typically appear when a relation has overlapping composite candidate keys. Learning to spot those cases, and to decompose the relation into projections that preserve the original data through a lossless join, is the core skill of this topic.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, what are the candidate keys of the example relation, and which dependency violates the superkey rule? Second, how is the decomposition constructed from the violating dependency, and why is the resulting join lossless? Third, what is lost in the trade: BCNF decomposition can fail to preserve all functional dependencies, which is exactly the tension between 3NF and BCNF that you will be asked to evaluate.

Comparing 3NF and BCNF

Introduction

Third normal form (3NF) and Boyce-Codd normal form (BCNF) are close cousins. Both attack the same problem — update, insertion, and deletion anomalies caused by functional dependencies on something other than a whole key — but they draw the line in slightly different places. 3NF allows a dependency whose left-hand side is not a superkey as long as the attribute on the right is prime (part of some candidate key); BCNF allows no such exception, requiring every non-trivial functional dependency to have a superkey on the left. For the large majority of schemas, these two definitions select the same decompositions, and the distinction never comes up in practice.

The interesting cases are the ones where they diverge. The classic gap appears when a relation has multiple overlapping candidate keys — the textbook examples usually involve something like a course, an instructor, and a time slot. There, the schema can be in 3NF yet still carry redundancy, and pushing it to BCNF forces you to split a relation in a way that leaves some functional dependency spanning two tables, so it can no longer be checked without a join. That is the trade-off at the heart of this topic: BCNF buys you stronger redundancy elimination, while 3NF guarantees that a dependency-preserving decomposition always exists. Both forms, importantly, can always be achieved with a lossless join.

As you watch, keep three questions in front of you for every example: what are all the candidate keys, which functional dependencies have a non-superkey on the left, and after a proposed decomposition can each original dependency still be enforced inside a single relation? Note also that normalization is a design judgment, not a rule to apply blindly — be ready to argue when stopping at 3NF is the right engineering choice and when the redundancy BCNF removes is worth the cost of enforcing a dependency across tables.

Multivalued Dependencies and Fourth Normal Form (4NF)

Introduction

Functional dependencies drive normalization up through Boyce-Codd Normal Form, but they do not capture every kind of redundancy. A multivalued dependency (MVD) arises when one attribute determines a set of values for another attribute independently of the rest of the relation. The classic symptom is a table that stores two unrelated repeating facts about the same key — for example, an instructor's courses and the instructor's assigned textbooks or hobbies — forcing you to store every combination of the two sets. The table can be in BCNF (its only functional dependency may be the trivial one on the full key) and still be full of repeated rows that cause update anomalies.

Fourth Normal Form addresses exactly this situation. A relation is in 4NF if it is in BCNF and, for every nontrivial multivalued dependency X ->> Y, X is a superkey. The repair is decomposition: split the relation so each independent multivalued fact lives in its own table, which by Fagin's theorem produces a lossless join. In this course, 4NF sits between BCNF and 5NF in the sequence you will apply to a complete schema design, so you need to recognize when functional dependency analysis alone has stopped being enough.

As you watch the videos below, focus on three things: how an MVD is stated and how it differs from a functional dependency, what the sample table looks like before and after decomposition, and whether the join of the decomposed tables reproduces the original rows exactly. Also note where presenters disagree about how often 4NF matters in practice — that debate is part of the trade-off discussion for this topic.

Join Dependencies and Fifth Normal Form (5NF)

Introduction

A join dependency is the most general of the dependency types we study in this course. It says that a relation can be reconstructed exactly by joining several of its projections — no rows lost, no spurious rows added. Functional dependencies and multivalued dependencies are both special cases of this idea; a multivalued dependency is simply a join dependency that splits a relation into exactly two projections. Fifth Normal Form, sometimes called Project-Join Normal Form, requires that every non-trivial join dependency in a relation be implied by its candidate keys.

The practical question 5NF answers is this: when a relation holds a three-way (or wider) association — supplier, part, project; instructor, course, semester; agent, company, product — can it be split into two-column pairs without losing information? Sometimes yes, and the original table carries redundancy that should be removed. Sometimes no, and any two-way split produces a join that invents facts that were never recorded. Learning to tell these cases apart, usually by testing whether the join of the projections reproduces the original rows, is the core skill of this topic.

As you watch, keep three things in view. First, note how each presenter builds the example table and then performs the join to test for a lossless decomposition — that test is what you will be asked to reproduce on assignments. Second, watch for how 5NF is distinguished from 4NF, since the two look similar but rest on different foundations. Third, pay attention to any discussion of cost: 5NF decompositions can require three-way joins to answer ordinary queries, so designers do not always apply it, and you should be able to argue when the trade-off is worth making.

Applying Higher Normal Forms to Complex Schemas

Introduction

So far in this module you have studied BCNF, 4NF, and 5NF as definitions. This topic shifts the focus from definitions to practice: how do you take a messy, real-world schema with many attributes, overlapping candidate keys, and poorly documented business rules, and systematically work it into a higher normal form? The process usually starts with an audit — listing attributes, inferring the functional dependencies that actually hold in the data, identifying candidate keys, and only then testing each dependency against the BCNF condition that every determinant must be a superkey.

From there, normalization becomes a sequence of decisions rather than a single step. You decompose for BCNF, then check whether independent multi-valued facts have been forced into the same relation (a 4NF violation), then consider whether any remaining relation can be reconstructed from three or more projections (a 5NF join dependency). Each step must preserve the lossless-join property, and some steps cost you dependency preservation — a trade-off you have to justify, not ignore.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to two things. First, the mechanics: how a determinant that is not a superkey is pulled out into its own relation, and how the original relation is rewritten. Second, the judgment calls: when a designer chooses to stop at 3NF, when denormalization is defensible, and how the overall design process (requirements, entities, keys, refinement) frames the normalization work you do on paper.
