Introduction

The Entity-Relationship (ER) model is a conceptual tool for describing what data a system must store and how the pieces of that data relate to one another, before any decisions are made about tables, columns, or storage. It gives designers a small, standardized vocabulary — entities, attributes, relationships, cardinality, and participation — plus a diagram notation that lets a technical team and a non-technical client look at the same picture and agree on what the database is supposed to represent.

This matters in COP4708 because the ER diagram is the bridge between a written problem description and a working relational schema. If the conceptual model misses an entity, mislabels an attribute, or gets a cardinality backwards, those errors propagate into the logical design and eventually into the database itself. Learning to read, build, and critique ER diagrams is therefore a prerequisite skill for everything that follows in the design process.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, how each presenter decides what becomes an entity versus what becomes an attribute — this judgment call is the heart of conceptual modeling. Second, how attributes are classified and how the key attribute is identified and marked. Third, how relationships are named and how cardinality (1:1, 1:N, M:N) is written on the diagram. Also notice that notation varies: Chen-style notation uses rectangles, ovals, and diamonds, while crow's-foot style packs attributes inside the entity box. Both encode the same ideas, so focus on the underlying concepts rather than memorizing one drawing style.
