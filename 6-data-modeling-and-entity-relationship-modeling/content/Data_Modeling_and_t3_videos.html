Introduction

Entities rarely exist in isolation. A student enrolls in a course, an order contains products, an employee reports to another employee — each of these is a relationship, an association between entity types that the data model must capture. This topic examines how those associations are described precisely enough to become part of a database design.

Three ideas do most of the work here. Cardinality states how many instances of one entity may be associated with instances of another (one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many). Participation constraints state whether that association is mandatory (total) or optional (partial) for each side. Degree describes how many entity types take part in the relationship — most are binary, but ternary and recursive (self-referencing) relationships also appear, as when an employee supervises other employees. Getting these details right matters because they determine how the model is later translated into tables, keys, and foreign keys; a many-to-many relationship, for example, cannot be implemented directly and requires a bridge (associative) table.

As you watch the videos, pay attention to notation. Chen notation draws relationships as diamonds with labels, while Crow's Foot notation encodes cardinality and participation in the line endings themselves. Watch for how each notation shows "one" versus "many" and "optional" versus "mandatory," and try to restate each diagram in a plain English sentence — for example, "each order must be placed by exactly one customer, and a customer may place zero or more orders." If you can move between the sentence and the diagram in both directions, you understand the relationship.
