Introduction to Data Modeling

Introduction

Data modeling is the practice of describing the data an organization cares about — the things it tracks, the facts it stores about those things, and the connections between them — before any tables are created in a database system. Think of it as the architectural drawing that comes before construction: it lets you reason about structure, catch problems, and agree with stakeholders on meaning while changes are still cheap.

In this course, data modeling is the bridge between a written problem description and a working relational database. It matters because design decisions made at the modeling stage ripple through everything that follows: query complexity, data integrity, redundancy, and how easily the system can grow. The topic also introduces the idea of levels of abstraction — conceptual, logical, and physical — which gives you a vocabulary for talking about the same data at different degrees of detail and independence from any particular DBMS.

As you watch the videos below, look for three things. First, note how each presenter defines a model and what they say it is for. Second, pay close attention to where the boundary falls between conceptual, logical, and physical descriptions, and what information is added at each step. Third, watch how modeling fits into an overall design workflow — where requirements come in and where implementation begins.

Relationships Between Entities

Introduction

Entities rarely exist in isolation. A student enrolls in a course, an order contains products, an employee reports to another employee — each of these is a relationship, an association between entity types that the data model must capture. This topic examines how those associations are described precisely enough to become part of a database design.

Three ideas do most of the work here. Cardinality states how many instances of one entity may be associated with instances of another (one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many). Participation constraints state whether that association is mandatory (total) or optional (partial) for each side. Degree describes how many entity types take part in the relationship — most are binary, but ternary and recursive (self-referencing) relationships also appear, as when an employee supervises other employees. Getting these details right matters because they determine how the model is later translated into tables, keys, and foreign keys; a many-to-many relationship, for example, cannot be implemented directly and requires a bridge (associative) table.

As you watch the videos, pay attention to notation. Chen notation draws relationships as diamonds with labels, while Crow's Foot notation encodes cardinality and participation in the line endings themselves. Watch for how each notation shows "one" versus "many" and "optional" versus "mandatory," and try to restate each diagram in a plain English sentence — for example, "each order must be placed by exactly one customer, and a customer may place zero or more orders." If you can move between the sentence and the diagram in both directions, you understand the relationship.

The Entity-Relationship (ER) Model

Introduction

The Entity-Relationship (ER) model is a conceptual tool for describing what data a system must store and how the pieces of that data relate to one another, before any decisions are made about tables, columns, or storage. It gives designers a small, standardized vocabulary — entities, attributes, relationships, cardinality, and participation — plus a diagram notation that lets a technical team and a non-technical client look at the same picture and agree on what the database is supposed to represent.

This matters in COP4708 because the ER diagram is the bridge between a written problem description and a working relational schema. If the conceptual model misses an entity, mislabels an attribute, or gets a cardinality backwards, those errors propagate into the logical design and eventually into the database itself. Learning to read, build, and critique ER diagrams is therefore a prerequisite skill for everything that follows in the design process.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, how each presenter decides what becomes an entity versus what becomes an attribute — this judgment call is the heart of conceptual modeling. Second, how attributes are classified and how the key attribute is identified and marked. Third, how relationships are named and how cardinality (1:1, 1:N, M:N) is written on the diagram. Also notice that notation varies: Chen-style notation uses rectangles, ovals, and diamonds, while crow's-foot style packs attributes inside the entity box. Both encode the same ideas, so focus on the underlying concepts rather than memorizing one drawing style.

Creating ER Diagrams

Introduction

An entity-relationship (ER) diagram is a picture of the data a system needs to store. It shows the things we care about (entities), the facts we record about them (attributes), and how those things are connected (relationships). Because the diagram is built before any tables exist, it lets you and your stakeholders agree on the structure of the data while changes are still cheap.

In this module the ER diagram is the bridge between a written problem description and an actual database. You will practice reading a scenario, pulling out the nouns that become entities, deciding which details belong as attributes, drawing the relationships, and then labeling each relationship with cardinality (one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many) and participation (whether every instance must take part). Notation matters here: Chen-style and crow's foot notation express the same ideas with different symbols, so be sure you can read both and stay consistent within a single diagram.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, how the presenter decides what becomes an entity versus what becomes an attribute. Second, how cardinality symbols are placed and read at each end of a relationship line. Third, how many-to-many relationships are handled, since that choice is where conceptual diagrams begin to turn into something implementable. Try pausing and sketching the examples yourself before the answer appears on screen.

Applying ER Modeling to a Problem Domain

Introduction

Up to this point you have learned the vocabulary of ER modeling: entities, attributes, keys, relationships, cardinality, and participation. This topic is where that vocabulary gets put to work. Given a messy, informal description of a business — a library, a clinic, an online store — your job is to read it carefully, decide what the system actually needs to keep track of, and turn that into a diagram precise enough that someone else could build tables from it.

The work usually follows a repeatable path. You analyze the requirements and underline the nouns and verbs that matter, propose a candidate list of entities, decide which details belong to which entity as attributes, name the relationships that connect entities, and then pin down how many of one relate to how many of the other and whether participation is optional or mandatory. Only then do you draw the full diagram, and even then you are not finished: a good designer re-reads the original description and checks the model against it, looking for duplicated data, missing entities, or relationships that were assumed rather than stated.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to the reasoning rather than just the finished picture. Notice how a noun in a sentence becomes either an entity or an attribute depending on how much detail the business needs, how a many-to-many relationship is handled, and what kinds of errors experienced designers watch out for. Try pausing each video after the requirements are stated and sketching your own diagram before seeing the presenter's version — comparing the two is where most of the learning happens.
