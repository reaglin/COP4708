Introduction to ER Diagrams and Relational Schemas

Introduction

Before you can build a working relational database, you need a plan. An Entity-Relationship (ER) diagram is that plan: a visual model that shows the things a system stores information about (entities), the facts recorded about them (attributes), and how those things connect to one another (relationships). A relational schema, by contrast, is the concrete result: the set of tables, columns, primary keys, and foreign keys that a database management system actually implements.

This topic gives you the vocabulary and notation you will rely on for the rest of the module. You will meet entity types and entity instances, simple versus composite and multivalued attributes, key attributes, cardinality and participation constraints, and the distinction between strong entities and weak entities — entities that cannot be identified on their own and depend on an owner entity for part of their identity. Every conversion rule later in the module refers back to these ideas, so time spent getting the terminology straight now pays off quickly.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: how each symbol or shape maps to a specific database concept, how cardinality (one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many) is expressed in the diagram, and where primary and foreign keys begin to appear. Try sketching along with the examples rather than only watching — recognizing notation is much easier than producing it, and this module will ask you to produce it.

Mapping Strong Entities and Attributes to Tables

Introduction

Mapping strong entities to tables is the first and most fundamental step in turning an ER diagram into a working relational schema. A strong entity is one that exists independently and has its own key attribute, so it becomes a table in its own right: each simple attribute becomes a column, and the key attribute becomes the primary key. Once you can do this reliably, the harder cases — weak entities, relationships, and junction tables — build directly on the same habits.

The details matter. Composite attributes such as Address are normally flattened into their component columns (Street, City, Zip) rather than stored as one blob. Multi-valued attributes such as PhoneNumbers cannot live in a single column at all; they require a separate table keyed in part by the owning entity's primary key. Derived attributes such as Age are usually left out of the table entirely and computed when needed, since storing them invites inconsistency.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running checklist: Which boxes in the diagram are entities? Which attribute is underlined (the key)? Which attributes are composite, multi-valued, or derived, and what does each case force you to do to the table design? Try sketching the resulting CREATE TABLE statements on paper as the videos talk through examples — the mapping rules stick much faster when you write them out.

Handling One-to-Many Relationships

Introduction

One-to-many is the workhorse relationship of relational database design. In an ER diagram it says that one instance of an entity can be associated with many instances of another, but each of those many is associated with at most one of the first — one department has many employees, but each employee belongs to one department. When you convert that diagram into tables, the relationship itself does not usually become its own table; instead it is captured by a foreign key.

The rule you will practice in this topic is short but easy to misapply: the foreign key goes on the 'many' side, referencing the primary key of the 'one' side. Putting it the other way around forces you either to repeat rows or to store multiple values in a single column, both of which break the relational model. You will also look at what participation constraints mean for the resulting column: total (mandatory) participation on the many side generally means the foreign key column is NOT NULL, while partial (optional) participation allows nulls.

As you watch the videos below, keep a pencil handy and sketch the tables yourself before the presenter reveals them. Watch specifically for (1) how each speaker decides which side is 'many', (2) where the foreign key column lands and what it is named, (3) how the primary key of the referenced table is reused as the foreign key's data type and domain, and (4) any mention of nullability or referential actions. If a video shows a tool or a particular SQL dialect, focus on the design reasoning rather than the exact syntax.

Handling Many-to-Many Relationships

Introduction

In an ER diagram, a many-to-many relationship says that each instance of entity A can be associated with many instances of entity B, and vice versa — a student enrolls in many courses, and each course enrolls many students. The relational model has no way to express this directly: a single foreign key column can only hold one value, so you cannot place a foreign key on either side without either losing data or repeating rows. The standard solution is a junction table (also called a bridge, associative, or link table) that sits between the two entity tables.

The junction table holds one foreign key column referencing each parent table's primary key. Together those foreign keys usually form a composite primary key, which guarantees that the same pairing is never recorded twice. If the relationship itself carries attributes — an enrollment grade, a quantity ordered, a start date — those columns belong in the junction table, because they describe the pairing rather than either entity alone. Mapping the ER diagram then becomes mechanical: two entity tables, one junction table, two foreign keys, one composite key, plus any relationship attributes.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in front of you. First, how does the presenter decide a relationship is truly many-to-many? Second, which columns become the primary key of the intermediate table, and why does a composite key solve the duplication problem? Third, where do relationship attributes end up? Note the differences in vocabulary between sources — "bridge table," "junction table," "link table," and "composite key" versus "multi-column primary key" — since you will meet all of these terms in practice.

Mapping Weak Entities and Their Relationships

Introduction

A weak entity is one that cannot be identified on its own. Its existence and its identity both depend on another entity — the owner, or identifying, entity. Classic examples include a Dependent that only makes sense in relation to an Employee, or an Apartment Unit that only makes sense within a particular Building. Because a weak entity has no attribute that is unique across the whole table, it carries only a partial key (sometimes called a discriminator): a value that distinguishes it among the other weak entities belonging to the same owner, but not globally.

When you map a weak entity to a relational table, you follow the usual steps — create a table, add the simple attributes as columns — and then do two extra things. First, bring in the primary key of the owner entity as a foreign key. Second, build the table's primary key as a composite of that foreign key plus the partial key. The identifying relationship itself does not become a separate table; it is absorbed into this foreign key. Finally, because the weak entity has no independent existence, the foreign key is normally declared NOT NULL and the deletion behavior is typically ON DELETE CASCADE, so removing an owner row removes its dependents rather than leaving orphans.

The videos below approach this from two directions. The Lucid Software ERD tutorials give you the diagram-side vocabulary — entities, attributes, keys, and how primary and foreign keys connect tables — while the kudvenkat video shows the actual SQL for declaring a primary key over two columns, which is exactly the mechanic you need for the weak entity's composite key. As you watch, keep translating: when you see a key being described in a diagram, ask yourself what the CREATE TABLE statement would look like, and when you see SQL, ask which part of the ER diagram produced it.

Validating and Refining the Relational Schema

Introduction

Translating an ER diagram into tables is only half the job. Once you have a draft schema, you need to review it deliberately: does every entity and relationship in the diagram appear somewhere in the tables? Does every table have a primary key that truly identifies one row? Does every foreign key point at an existing primary key, with a sensible rule for what happens on update or delete? Validation is the step where modeling mistakes are cheapest to fix — before data is loaded and before application code depends on the structure.

This topic walks through a repeatable review process: cross-check tables against ER entities and relationships, verify primary key integrity (including composite keys for junction tables and weak entities), validate foreign key constraints and referential integrity, enforce consistent naming conventions, apply normalization checks (typically through 3NF, sometimes BCNF), confirm that domain, NOT NULL, UNIQUE, and CHECK constraints are all present, and then iterate until the schema is stable.

As you watch the videos below, keep a draft schema in front of you and treat each one as a checklist source. In the first video, note which mistakes are detectable by inspecting a schema alone. In the normalization videos, focus on how you test a table for a given normal form rather than on memorizing definitions — the goal is to be able to look at one of your own tables and say precisely which dependency violates which rule, and how to decompose it.
