Introduction

Normalization is the systematic process of organizing the tables and columns of a relational database so that each fact is stored in exactly one place. When a design stores the same fact repeatedly — a customer's address repeated on every order row, for example — the database becomes vulnerable to inconsistency: one copy gets updated and the others do not. Normalization attacks this problem at the structural level by examining how attributes depend on keys and splitting tables until each table describes one kind of thing.

In this module you will work through the first three normal forms in detail, so this introductory topic sets up the vocabulary and the motivation you will need. Pay particular attention to the three classic anomalies — insertion, update, and deletion — because they are the practical symptoms that tell you a table is badly designed. Equally important is the idea of a functional dependency: the statement that one set of attributes determines another. Every rule you will learn for 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF is ultimately a statement about which dependencies are allowed to live in a table.

As you watch the videos below, look for three things: concrete examples of redundancy and the trouble it causes, the role of keys in deciding where an attribute belongs, and the reasoning behind each normal form rather than just its definition. The third video deliberately covers denormalization — the deliberate reintroduction of redundancy — so that you understand normalization as a design judgment with trade-offs, not a rule to be applied blindly.
