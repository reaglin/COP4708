When Does a Spreadsheet Stop Being Enough?

Prompt: A small community food co-op has run on one spreadsheet for three years. Now it has 900 members, three volunteers editing at the same time, supplier details entered four different ways, and no quick way to answer "how much did we buy from this farm last quarter?" The board insists the spreadsheet is "basically a database." Where would you draw the line? At what point does a collection of data stop being "just a spreadsheet" and start requiring a database and a DBMS — and what makes that line genuinely hard to draw?

Your initial post (about 150–250 words), posted early in the week, should:

  • State clearly where you would draw the line and name the single factor that matters most to you (for example, volume, simultaneous users, consistency of entries, or ease of retrieval).
  • Use the module's definitions of a database and a DBMS to support your position, and use the terms record, field, and table correctly at least once.
  • Illustrate your position with one concrete example — the co-op, or a data collection from your own studies, work, or community.
  • Name one thing that makes the line hard to draw, or one point on which you could see a reasonable person disagreeing with you.

Replies: Post two replies by the end of the week, and send at least one of them to a classmate who has not yet received a response. A good reply quotes or names the specific claim you are engaging, then does one of these: offers a counter-example that tests their threshold, supplies evidence or an example that strengthens it, or asks a question that would make them refine it. Replies that only say you agree do not count — add a reason.

Assessment: Posts are assessed on the clarity of your position, the accuracy of your use of module definitions and vocabulary, the quality of your example, and how substantively your replies advance a classmate's thinking.
