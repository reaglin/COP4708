One Table or Many? The Hidden Cost of Keeping Data Tidy

Prompt: Relational databases buy you something valuable — a defined schema, keys that uniquely identify and link records, and normalization that cuts redundancy — but you pay for it in up-front design work and in queries that have to join tables back together. When is that structure worth the cost, and when does it get in the way?

Your initial post (about 150–250 words), early in the week, should:

  • Take a position on the central question and state it in your first sentence.
  • Ground it in one concrete application — a clinic, an online store, a campus club, a banking system, a game, anything you know. A short invented scenario is fine; just label it as an example.
  • Sketch the tables you would use (two or three is plenty), name a primary key, and name where a foreign key would link them. Plain text is fine, e.g. Patients(patient_id, name) → Visits(visit_id, patient_id, date).
  • Say what would go wrong in your example without that structure — and name one real cost of having it.
  • Use the module's vocabulary precisely: table, row, column, schema, primary key, foreign key, normalization, SQL.

Your replies: Write two replies by the end of the week, to classmates who picked a different application or reached a different conclusion than you did. A good reply does one of three things: stress-tests their primary key with a situation that would break it, offers a case where their conclusion would not hold, or extends their design by adding a table or relationship they did not need yet — and always says why, with reference to the module's ideas. Replies like "great example, I agree" do not count. Return to your own thread before the week ends to answer questions others have asked you.

Assessment: Posts are assessed on the clarity of your position, the accuracy and precision of your use of relational vocabulary and keys, the concreteness of your example, and whether your replies genuinely advance a classmate's thinking.
