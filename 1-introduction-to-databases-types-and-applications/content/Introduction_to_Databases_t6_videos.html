Introduction

Almost every digital service you used today was backed by a database. When you browse a product catalog, check a bank balance, refill a prescription, submit an assignment, or scroll a social feed, software is reading from and writing to a data store that someone designed on purpose. This topic looks at that connection between database theory and the systems people actually rely on: e-commerce and retail, healthcare records, banking and financial services, social media and content platforms, education and learning management systems, government and public services, and transportation and logistics.

The reason this matters in COP4708 is that different industries place very different demands on their data. A bank cannot tolerate a transfer that debits one account without crediting another, so it leans on strict transactional guarantees. A social platform serves enormous volumes of reads and can often accept slightly stale data in exchange for speed and scale. A hospital must keep records accurate, auditable, and available. Those pressures — correctness, scale, flexibility, and reporting needs — are what push designers toward relational, NoSQL, or other models.

The three videos below come from ByteByteGo, a channel focused on system design. Rather than touring industries one by one, they give you the vocabulary that explains why industries choose what they choose: transactional guarantees, core architectural building blocks, and the pipelines that move data from operational systems into analytics. As you watch, keep asking yourself which industry from the subtopic list would care most about each idea, and why.

Video 1: ACID Properties in Databases With Examples

ByteByteGo · 4:57 · 309,562 views · 2y ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

This video explains the ACID properties — atomicity, consistency, isolation, and durability — that define how reliable database transactions behave, and it works through examples to make each property concrete. Expect scenarios along the lines of money transfers or order processing, where a partially completed operation would leave the data in a broken state. It should also make clear why these guarantees are a feature of transactional database engines rather than something application code can easily fake.

Why this video

ACID is the single clearest explanation for why banking, financial services, healthcare records, and e-commerce checkout systems overwhelmingly rely on transactional relational databases, which makes this a direct fit for the industry-matching subtopics.

This video surveys eight widely used system design concepts, the kind of building blocks that appear in large-scale production architectures. Why this video
