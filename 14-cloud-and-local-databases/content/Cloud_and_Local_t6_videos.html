Introduction

Every database, no matter where it runs, needs ongoing care: operating system and engine patches, index and storage maintenance, backups, restore testing, performance tuning, capacity planning, and security configuration. What changes between a locally hosted deployment and a cloud-managed service is not whether this work happens, but who does it. In a self-managed environment your organization owns nearly all of it and must staff accordingly; with a managed service such as AWS RDS, Azure SQL Database, or Google Cloud SQL, the provider absorbs much of the infrastructure-level work while you remain accountable for schema design, query performance, access control, and your own data.

This topic matters because administrative burden is one of the most under-estimated cost and risk factors in a hosting decision. A team may choose the cloud expecting "no maintenance" and then discover that backup retention, patch windows, encryption keys, and user privileges are still theirs to manage. The shared responsibility model is the framework that makes those boundaries explicit, and understanding it is essential before you can defend a hosting recommendation in a real scenario.

As you watch, keep a two-column list: tasks the provider performs versus tasks the customer retains, and note how that line shifts as you move from IaaS toward PaaS and SaaS. Pay attention to the distinction between a backup and a recovery plan, and to what monitoring and expertise each model demands from your staff.
