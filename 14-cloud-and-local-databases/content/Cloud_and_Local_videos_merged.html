Introduction to Database Deployment Models

Introduction

A database deployment model describes where a database actually runs and who is responsible for keeping it running. In a locally hosted (on-premises) model, your organization owns or leases the physical servers, storage, and network gear, installs the database software, and handles patching, backups, tuning, and physical security. In a cloud-based model, a provider such as AWS, Microsoft Azure, or Google Cloud supplies the infrastructure and, in managed Database-as-a-Service offerings, much of the routine administration as well.

This distinction matters in COP4708 because almost every design decision later in the module depends on it: how you pay for capacity, how you scale under load, how you achieve availability and disaster recovery, and which security and compliance tasks remain yours. Deployment choice is rarely about which model is technically 'better' — it is about matching infrastructure characteristics to an organization's budget, staffing, regulatory environment, and growth expectations.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running comparison across a few key dimensions: cost structure (upfront capital spending versus ongoing operational spending), scalability, maintenance effort, availability, security, and control. Also pay attention to the division of labor — note each time a speaker mentions a task the provider handles versus one the customer still owns, since that idea becomes the shared responsibility model later in the module.

Locally Hosted Database Systems

Introduction

A locally hosted (on-premises) database system runs on hardware that your organization owns or leases and physically controls — servers in a rack, in a closet, or in a data center you rent space in. Everything from the power supply and network cabling to the operating system, the database engine, patching, backups, and user security sits with your team. That total ownership is the defining characteristic of local deployment, and it is the baseline against which every cloud database service in this module is compared.

This matters in COP4708 because deployment choice shapes real decisions: how you budget (large up-front capital purchases plus ongoing staffing, versus metered monthly bills), how you scale (buying and installing more hardware, versus resizing an instance in a console), and who is accountable when a disk fails at 2 a.m. Local deployment is still the right answer in many situations — strict data residency rules, low-latency industrial or lab workloads, legacy applications, and environments with poor or no internet connectivity — so understanding it well is not just historical background.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running list of tasks that a human has to perform on a local system: provisioning hardware, installing and configuring software, monitoring, applying updates, arranging redundancy, and testing recovery. Note where each of those tasks goes when the same workload moves to a managed cloud service. Also pay attention to cost: notice which costs are visible (hardware, licenses) and which are hidden (staff time, downtime, spare capacity bought "just in case").

Cloud-Based Database Services

Introduction

A managed cloud database service is a database that runs on a provider's infrastructure, where the provider handles most of the underlying work: provisioning servers, installing and patching the database engine, taking backups, and keeping replicas available. You still design schemas, write queries, tune indexes, and control access, but you no longer rack hardware or babysit an operating system. The three services named in this topic — Amazon RDS, Azure SQL Database, and Google Cloud SQL — are the flagship relational offerings of the three largest public cloud providers, and they share far more in common than their marketing names suggest.

This matters in COP4708 because the choice between a locally hosted database and a cloud-based one changes how you budget, how you scale, who is responsible when something breaks, and how clients reach the database over a network. Managed services shift costs from large up-front purchases to recurring usage charges, replace manual capacity planning with resizing and read replicas, and divide administrative duties between you and the provider under a shared responsibility model. Understanding where that dividing line falls is the practical skill this topic builds.

As you watch the videos below, listen for a few recurring ideas: which tasks the provider automates versus which remain yours; how backups, replication, and high availability are described; how elasticity and pay-as-you-go pricing are framed; and how applications connect to a cloud database through endpoints, networking rules, and credentials. Also note that the videos come from IBM and AWS, so vendor terminology will appear — try to map each vendor-specific term back to the general concept it represents, since the same concept exists under a different name on Azure and Google Cloud.

Cost Comparison: Cloud vs. Local

Introduction

Choosing between a cloud-hosted and a locally hosted database is rarely just a technical decision — it is a financial one. A local deployment usually means buying servers, storage, and networking gear up front, paying for perpetual or per-core database licenses, and staffing people to patch, back up, and monitor the system. A cloud deployment shifts most of that into a recurring bill that scales with what you consume, but that bill never ends and can grow quickly if workloads are not sized and managed carefully.

This topic gives you the vocabulary and the analytical frame for that comparison: capital expenditure (CapEx) versus operational expenditure (OpEx), hardware and refresh cycles, licensing differences between on-premises and cloud editions, ongoing operational and maintenance labor, and how elasticity changes the cost-efficiency equation. The goal is Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) thinking — accounting for every cost over a multi-year horizon rather than comparing a server price tag to a monthly subscription.

As you watch the videos below, listen for three things: how each cost is classified (one-time versus recurring), who absorbs the cost under each model, and what assumptions drive the answer — workload steadiness, utilization rates, growth rate, and staffing. Those assumptions are usually what decide whether cloud or local comes out cheaper for a given organization, and you will be expected to state them when you make a recommendation.

Scalability and Performance

Introduction

Scalability is the ability of a database system to keep meeting performance expectations as data volume, user counts, and query complexity grow. Performance is the related but distinct question of how quickly the system responds under a given load. In this topic we look at both through the lens of deployment model: a locally hosted database is bounded by the hardware you bought and the space, power, and procurement cycle around it, while a cloud database service can often add capacity on demand, sometimes automatically, and bill you for what you use.

This matters in COP4708 because scaling decisions are where the architectural differences between on-premises and cloud deployments become concrete. Vertical scaling (a bigger machine), horizontal scaling (more machines), read replicas, caching, indexing, partitioning, and sharding are techniques available in both worlds, but the cost, speed, and operational burden of applying them differ sharply. Elasticity in the cloud is attractive, yet it is not free: pooled or autoscaling resources introduce their own cost models, limits, and tuning decisions.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, which scaling techniques require application or schema changes and which are purely infrastructure moves? Second, for each technique, what would it take to implement on a server in your own data center versus on a managed cloud service? Third, where does the money go — what are you paying for when you scale up, scale out, or pool resources, and at what point does the trade-off favor one deployment model over the other?

Maintenance and Administration

Introduction

Every database, no matter where it runs, needs ongoing care: operating system and engine patches, index and storage maintenance, backups, restore testing, performance tuning, capacity planning, and security configuration. What changes between a locally hosted deployment and a cloud-managed service is not whether this work happens, but who does it. In a self-managed environment your organization owns nearly all of it and must staff accordingly; with a managed service such as AWS RDS, Azure SQL Database, or Google Cloud SQL, the provider absorbs much of the infrastructure-level work while you remain accountable for schema design, query performance, access control, and your own data.

This topic matters because administrative burden is one of the most under-estimated cost and risk factors in a hosting decision. A team may choose the cloud expecting "no maintenance" and then discover that backup retention, patch windows, encryption keys, and user privileges are still theirs to manage. The shared responsibility model is the framework that makes those boundaries explicit, and understanding it is essential before you can defend a hosting recommendation in a real scenario.

As you watch, keep a two-column list: tasks the provider performs versus tasks the customer retains, and note how that line shifts as you move from IaaS toward PaaS and SaaS. Pay attention to the distinction between a backup and a recovery plan, and to what monitoring and expertise each model demands from your staff.

Availability, Reliability, and Security

Introduction

Choosing where a database lives is not only a question of cost or convenience — it is a question of how much downtime an organization can tolerate, how quickly it can recover when something fails, and who is accountable when data is exposed. This topic brings those concerns together under three headings: availability (will the database respond when users need it), reliability (can it survive hardware failure, human error, or a regional outage), and security (who can reach the data, and how is it protected at rest and in transit).

In a cloud deployment, much of this is expressed contractually through a Service Level Agreement, and technically through provider features such as redundant replicas, automated backups, and geo-replication. In a local deployment, the same guarantees have to be engineered and funded by the organization itself: backup schedules, standby servers, off-site copies, failover testing, and physical and network security controls. Neither model removes the work — the cloud shifts part of it to the provider under a shared responsibility model, while on-premises keeps all of it in house.

As you watch the videos, look for three things. First, the vocabulary: RTO (recovery time objective), RPO (recovery point objective), high availability versus disaster recovery, failover, and uptime percentages. Second, the mechanisms: what specifically provides redundancy in each model. Third, the division of labor: note each time a task is performed automatically by a provider versus performed manually by a DBA, because that distinction is the heart of the shared responsibility model and of the recommendations you will be asked to make later in this module.
