Review of Normalization Principles

Introduction

Normalization is the process of organizing the columns and tables of a relational database so that each fact is stored in exactly one place. It is driven by functional dependencies — statements of the form "if you know the value of A, you know the value of B" — and it proceeds through a series of normal forms: 1NF removes repeating groups and non-atomic values, 2NF removes partial dependencies on part of a composite key, 3NF removes transitive dependencies on non-key attributes, and BCNF tightens 3NF by requiring that every determinant be a candidate key.

In this module, normalization is the baseline you will later deliberately depart from. Before you can argue that denormalizing a schema is worth the write overhead and storage cost, you need to be able to say precisely what normal form a table is in and which anomalies — insertion, update, and deletion — you are accepting when you relax it. That is why this topic revisits 1NF through BCNF and the dependency theory underneath them rather than treating them as rules to memorize.

As you watch, keep a worked example of your own in front of you. For each table discussed, try to write down the candidate keys first, then list the functional dependencies, then check each one against the definition of the normal form being explained. Pay particular attention to the difference between 3NF and BCNF — the gap between them is small, appears only in tables with overlapping candidate keys, and is the detail students most often get wrong on exams.

Normalization in a Practical Context

Introduction

Normalization is usually introduced as a set of rules: identify functional dependencies, remove partial and transitive dependencies, and work your way up through 1NF, 2NF, 3NF, and BCNF. In practice, you rarely start with a messy table and normalize it in isolation. You start with requirements, draw an entity-relationship model, and then produce a relational schema whose tables are already close to normalized because the entities and relationships were identified well in the first place. This topic connects those two views: the theory you can apply as a check, and the design process that produces well-structured schemas in the first place.

Real-world data complicates things. Business rules change, source data arrives denormalized from spreadsheets or legacy systems, some dependencies are ambiguous until you talk to a domain expert, and splitting tables for correctness can mean more joins at query time. Design is therefore iterative: you sketch a model, test it against sample data and expected queries, discover a dependency you missed, and revise. This module's later topics on denormalization build directly on this foundation — you cannot make a sound trade-off decision unless you know what the normalized baseline looks like.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to how each presenter moves from a business description to entities, attributes, keys, and relationships. Ask yourself where a functional dependency is being implicitly resolved when an attribute is placed in one table rather than another, how many-to-many relationships become junction tables, and where the presenter makes a judgment call rather than following a mechanical rule. Those judgment calls are exactly what this topic is about.

Introduction to Denormalization

Introduction

Up to this point in the module, the goal has been to remove redundancy: decompose tables, eliminate update anomalies, and push a schema toward 3NF or BCNF. Denormalization appears to reverse that work, but it is not an undoing of normalization. It is a deliberate, documented decision to reintroduce specific redundancy — a duplicated column, a precomputed total, a merged table, a summary table — in order to make certain queries faster or simpler, while accepting the extra work required to keep that redundancy correct.

The distinction that matters most in this course is between redundancy that happens by accident and redundancy that is chosen on purpose. Accidental redundancy is a design flaw; you cannot explain why it is there and you cannot say which copy is authoritative. Controlled redundancy is a strategy: you know exactly which normalized form the data would take, you know which anomalies you are now exposed to, and you have a plan — triggers, application logic, batch refresh — for keeping the copies in sync. This is why denormalization is taught after normalization and never before it. You cannot responsibly denormalize a schema you could not normalize.

While watching these videos, keep the core trade-off in view: denormalization usually buys read performance and pays for it with write complexity, storage, and risk of inconsistency. Listen for the conditions the presenters attach to their advice — what the workload looks like, how often the data changes, whether the duplicated value can be recomputed. Note also any alternatives they mention (indexes, materialized views, caching), since a good designer tries those before changing the logical schema.

Denormalization Techniques and Patterns

Introduction

Normalization gives us schemas that store each fact exactly once, which protects data integrity and makes updates simple. Denormalization is the deliberate, selective reversal of some of those decisions in order to make certain queries faster. The key word is deliberate: you denormalize after you have a normalized design, after you know your workload, and after you have measured that joins or repeated computation are actually a bottleneck.

This topic surveys the standard patterns. Storing a precomputed or derived value (an order total, an age, a full name) avoids recomputing it on every read. Merging tables, or table collapsing, folds a one-to-one or rarely-separate relationship into a single table to eliminate a join. Adding a redundant column copies an attribute from a parent table into a child so a lookup join disappears. Summary and aggregate tables materialize counts, sums, and rollups for reporting. Vertical partitioning splits a wide table by column usage, which is technically the opposite move but belongs to the same family of physical-design trade-offs.

As you watch, keep a running ledger of costs. Every redundant copy creates an update anomaly risk, and you must name the mechanism that keeps the copy correct: application code, a trigger, a materialized view refresh, or a scheduled batch job. Also notice how each video frames the read/write balance, since the right pattern for a read-heavy analytics workload is usually wrong for a write-heavy transactional one.

Performance Implications of Normalization vs. Denormalization

Introduction

Normalization and denormalization are not simply "right" and "wrong" ways to design a schema — they are two ends of a trade-off spectrum. A highly normalized schema stores each fact exactly once, which keeps writes small, cheap, and safe from update anomalies, but it scatters related data across many tables so that answering a question often requires several joins. A denormalized schema pre-combines or pre-computes that data so reads are fast, but the same fact now lives in multiple places, meaning every write must touch more rows and the risk of inconsistency rises. Storage follows a similar pattern: redundancy costs disk space, and summary tables and extra indexes cost even more.

This topic matters in COP4708 because design decisions are only defensible when you can tie them to a workload. Before choosing a degree of normalization you need to profile how the application actually behaves: what fraction of operations are reads versus writes, which queries are on the critical path, how often data changes, and how large the tables will grow. The same schema can be excellent for a reporting workload and terrible for a transaction-heavy one. The goal is a repeatable framework — measure the workload, estimate read cost, write cost, and storage cost for each candidate design, and justify the choice explicitly.

As you watch the videos below, look for three things. First, note how each presenter characterizes the cost of a join versus the cost of maintaining redundant data. Second, watch for the vocabulary of workload profiling — read-heavy, write-heavy, OLTP, analytics — and how that vocabulary drives the recommendation. Third, pay attention to indexing, caching, and replication as alternatives to denormalization; a performance problem does not always require changing the schema, and knowing the other options is part of making a defensible design decision.

Balancing Theoretical Purity with Real-World Needs

Introduction

Normalization gives you a rigorous, theory-backed way to eliminate redundancy and protect data integrity. But production systems rarely live at a single point on that scale. Real designs sit somewhere along a spectrum: some tables are kept in strict 3NF or BCNF because correctness and frequent updates demand it, while others carry precomputed totals, merged attributes, or summary rows because the read workload would otherwise be too slow or too expensive. Knowing where on that spectrum a given table belongs is a design skill, not a rule you memorize.

This topic matters in COP4708 because it connects everything you have practiced so far — functional dependencies, normal forms, ER-to-relational mapping — to the questions an engineer actually gets asked: How will this be queried? How often is it written? How large will it get? What breaks first when traffic grows tenfold? Denormalization is only defensible when you can name the workload pattern it serves and the cost you are accepting in write overhead, storage, or update anomalies. Without that reasoning, it is just a shortcut that creates bugs later.

The three videos below approach the problem from different angles: common design mistakes and how to avoid them, a structured way to choose the right data store for a workload, and the fundamental contrast between relational and NoSQL models. While watching, keep a running list of the decision criteria each speaker uses — read-vs-write ratio, data volume, consistency requirements, query shape, growth expectations. Those criteria are the raw material for the decision framework you will apply in this module, and they are also the basis for revisiting a design later when the workload changes.
