Introduction

Normalization is the process of organizing the columns and tables of a relational database so that each fact is stored in exactly one place. It is driven by functional dependencies — statements of the form "if you know the value of A, you know the value of B" — and it proceeds through a series of normal forms: 1NF removes repeating groups and non-atomic values, 2NF removes partial dependencies on part of a composite key, 3NF removes transitive dependencies on non-key attributes, and BCNF tightens 3NF by requiring that every determinant be a candidate key.

In this module, normalization is the baseline you will later deliberately depart from. Before you can argue that denormalizing a schema is worth the write overhead and storage cost, you need to be able to say precisely what normal form a table is in and which anomalies — insertion, update, and deletion — you are accepting when you relax it. That is why this topic revisits 1NF through BCNF and the dependency theory underneath them rather than treating them as rules to memorize.

As you watch, keep a worked example of your own in front of you. For each table discussed, try to write down the candidate keys first, then list the functional dependencies, then check each one against the definition of the normal form being explained. Pay particular attention to the difference between 3NF and BCNF — the gap between them is small, appears only in tables with overlapping candidate keys, and is the detail students most often get wrong on exams.
