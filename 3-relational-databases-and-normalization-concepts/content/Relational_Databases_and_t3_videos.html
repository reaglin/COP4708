Introduction

An insertion anomaly happens when the design of a table prevents you from recording a fact you legitimately want to store, because the table also demands unrelated information that does not yet exist. A classic example is a table that mixes two different kinds of entities — say, courses and the instructors assigned to them. If the table's primary key requires an instructor, you cannot record a newly approved course until someone is hired to teach it. The problem is not the data; it is the structure.

In this module, insertion anomalies are one of three related symptoms (alongside update and deletion anomalies) that signal unwanted dependencies in a schema. Watch for the ways designers try to work around the problem rather than fix it: leaving columns NULL, or inventing placeholder rows such as "TBD" or instructor ID 0000. These workarounds look harmless but corrode data integrity, since NULLs and dummy values propagate into queries, joins, constraints, and reports. The real fix is decomposition — splitting the table so each table describes one logical entity, with dependencies pointing only at the whole key.

As you watch the videos below, keep two questions in mind. First, which functional dependency in the example table is the culprit — what determines what, and is that determinant actually a key? Second, after the table is decomposed, which rows could now be inserted independently that could not be inserted before? Being able to narrate that before-and-after story is exactly what the module outcomes ask of you.

Video 2: Learn Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)

Decomplexify · 13:37 · 178,464 views · 4y ago · captions — Watch on YouTube

About this video

Decomplexify walks through Boyce-Codd Normal Form with worked examples, building up from the idea of functional dependencies to the rule that every determinant must be a candidate key. Expect the video to show a table that violates BCNF, identify the offending dependency, and then decompose the table into well-formed pieces. The examples are the kind where anomalies — including the inability to insert certain facts — are used to demonstrate that something is structurally wrong.

Why this video

This is the clearest widely used explanation of how an unwanted dependency produces anomalies and how decomposition removes them, which maps directly onto the subtopics on unwanted dependencies and normalization as the solution. Decomplexify's step-by-step, example-driven style makes the abstract rule concrete enough to apply to your own schemas.
