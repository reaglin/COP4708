Introduction

When a single table is asked to store too much at once — customers, orders, products, and salespeople all jammed together — the same facts end up repeated in row after row. That repetition is called data redundancy, and it is the root cause of the three classic problems you will study here: insertion anomalies (you cannot record one fact without inventing another), update anomalies (changing one fact requires finding and editing many rows, and missing one leaves the data contradictory), and deletion anomalies (removing one row accidentally destroys unrelated information).

This topic matters because it supplies the motivation for everything that follows in the module. Normalization can feel like an abstract set of rules until you have seen a concrete badly designed table and felt why it hurts. Once you can point at a schema and say "this column repeats, this table mixes two entity types, this fact depends on only part of the key," the normal forms stop being memorization and become a repair procedure.

As you watch, keep a simple checklist in mind: Which values repeat unnecessarily? If I changed one of those values, how many rows would I have to touch? What happens if I delete this row — do I lose something I still need? And what single real-world thing is this table actually about? Try pausing each video when a sample table appears and diagnosing it yourself before the narrator does.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos move from symptom to cure. Database Star's mistake catalog trains you to spot the warning signs — repeating groups, mixed entity types, overloaded columns — that MO3 targets, while the sample tables in all three videos reinforce the structural vocabulary of tables, rows, columns and keys from MO1. Gate Smashers' tour of the normal forms explains why each level of normalization exists by first exposing the insertion, update and deletion anomalies it removes, supporting MO2 and MO4. Tech Coach's worked exercise then gives you a model for MO5: taking one poorly structured table and decomposing it until each table describes a single logical entity.
